Operational vs Analytical

Most data systems sit on one side of a line. Either they run the product, or they study what the product did. Today you learn to tell which side a workload is on just from how it reads and writes data. Almost every later chapter of the book builds on this split.

One shop, two kinds of question

Picture an online bookshop. Backend engineers build the services that read and change data when someone uses the site. You open your order, and the app fetches that one order by its ID. You buy a book, and the app writes one new order.[1]

Two other groups also want the data. Business analysts write reports that help management decide things, such as "what was revenue per country last quarter?" Data scientists look for patterns and build features such as recommendations and spam filters. Both groups do analytics: they look at the data, but they usually don't change it.[1]

The book calls the first kind operational systems, also known as OLTP (online transaction processing). It calls the second kind analytical systems, also known as OLAP (online analytical processing).[1] Run all three requests below and watch which rows each one touches.

The seven differences

The lab showed the first two rows of this table. The book lists the rest.[1] You don't need to memorise it. The game in the next step will drill it.

Operational (OLTP)Analytical (OLAP)
Main readsPoint queries: fetch one record by its keyAggregate over a huge number of records
Main writesCreate, update or delete single recordsBulk import (ETL) or an event stream
Human userEnd user of the web or mobile appInternal analyst making business decisions
Machine userChecking whether an action is allowedDetecting fraud or abuse patterns
QueriesA fixed set, written into the appAnything the analyst thinks of
Data showsThe latest state, right nowHistory of events over time
Data sizeGigabytes to terabytesTerabytes to petabytes

"Online" in OLAP is fuzzy. The book reads it as: analysts explore interactively, instead of only running pre-written queries.[1]

Why keep them apart?

At first, companies ran reports on the same database the app used. From the late 1980s, they started moving analytics to a separate database called a data warehouse.[1] The book gives four reasons not to let analysts query the operational databases directly:[1]

  1. Silos. The data is spread over many operational systems: the website, payments, stock, delivery. A report often needs all of them.
  2. Wrong shape. An operational schema is built for point lookups, not for scanning and summarising.
  3. Cost. One big analytic query can slow the system down for everyone using the app.
  4. Access. Security and compliance rules may forbid analysts from reading operational databases.

Two things blur the line. Some databases, called HTAP (hybrid transactional/analytical processing), try to do both jobs. Many of them are really two engines behind one interface, and the book says they don't replace warehouses.[1] And some analytics runs inside the product, such as a "views per day" chart for a creator. The book calls this product analytics or real-time analytics.[1] How data moves from the app into a warehouse is level 1.2.

Say it in your own words

🎤 In the interview

When the interviewer adds "and the business wants dashboards" or "we want to detect fraud", say it out loud: that's an analytical workload. Then keep it off the request path. Serve users from the operational database, and copy the data somewhere built for scanning. That one sentence shows you know where the load goes.

🛠 At work

Think of the main database your team owns. Does anyone run reports, exports or BI dashboards against it directly? If so, which of the four reasons above is a risk for you? The Ch 1 side quest turns this into a diagram of your team's data.
